# How the US application actually works

You said you do not know this process well, so this is the ground the product is
standing on. It is written plainly and it is the basis for every judgement in
the other documents.

## The shape of a year

A US application is not one submission. It is a bundle assembled from four
different senders, and the student controls only one of them.

| Piece | Who sends it | When |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The application form and essays | The student | By the deadline |
| The school report and transcript | The school counsellor or registrar | By the deadline |
| Letters of recommendation | Teachers, directly | By the deadline |
| Test scores | The testing body, or the student | Varies |
| Financial aid forms | The family | Often a different, later deadline |

The student's own dashboard is therefore mostly a waiting room. Most of what
determines the outcome is in someone else's outbox. This is why "asked 26 days
ago" on a recommender is a genuinely useful line, and why nagging is a real
service rather than a nice touch.

## Deadlines, and why they are not one date

- **Early Decision (ED)**, usually 1 November. Binding. If admitted the student
  must attend and must withdraw every other application. One ED per student.
- **Early Decision II (ED2)**, usually 1 January. Same bond, later.
- **Early Action (EA)**, usually 1 or 15 November. Not binding.
- **Restrictive or Single Choice EA**, at a handful of colleges. Not binding but
  bars applying early elsewhere.
- **Regular Decision (RD)**, usually 1 or 15 January.
- **Rolling**, decided as they arrive, so earlier is materially better.

The binding nature of ED is the highest-stakes fact in the whole process and a
place where bad advice does real damage. A product that lets a student mark two
colleges as ED without objecting has a defect, not a missing feature.

## Essays

Two different things, often confused:

- **The personal statement.** One essay, 650 words, written once and sent to
  every college on the Common Application. This is the one that gets many
  rounds of editing.
- **Supplements.** Per college, often several, usually 100 to 400 words each.
  A student applying to twelve colleges can be facing thirty of these.

So rounds belong to the personal statement, and a count belongs to supplements.
Those are different units, which matters for how tiers are worded and priced.

## Recommendations, and FERPA

Typically one counsellor letter and two teacher letters. The student never sees
them and never handles the file. On the Common App the student adds the
recommender's email, the recommender gets a link, and the letter goes straight
to the colleges.

**The FERPA waiver** is the part people outside the process have not heard of.
Federal law gives an enrolled student the right to read their own education
records, including letters. The Common App asks the applicant to sign away that
right before inviting recommenders. Waiving is effectively compulsory in
practice: colleges weight an unwaived letter far less because the teacher knew
the student could read it, and many teachers refuse to write unless it is
waived. It is signed once, per application, before the invitations go out.

This is why a checkbox is not enough. The order matters: waive first, then
invite. A product that tracks the waiver after the fact has missed the point.

## Testing

Most colleges are now test optional, and a good score helps while a missing one
usually does not hurt. For a student whose first language is not English there
is a separate and non-optional requirement: **TOEFL, IELTS or Duolingo English
Test**. Nearly every US college demands one from an international applicant, and
minimum scores gate admission outright at many.

## Money

- **FAFSA**, the federal form. **Only United States citizens and eligible
  non-citizens may file it.** An Ethiopian student living in Ethiopia cannot.
- **CSS Profile**, run by the College Board, used by roughly 250 mostly private
  colleges to award their own money. **International students do use this one.**
- Many colleges also want their **own international aid form**, or the ISFAA,
  and will ask for proof of family income that does not look like a US tax
  return.

The distinction between need-blind and need-aware matters enormously here. Only
a handful of US colleges are need-blind for international applicants. At
everywhere else, asking for aid makes admission harder, and how much to ask for
is a strategic decision worth real money. That is advice, and advice is what a
counsellor is for.

## After the decision

- Accepted, denied, waitlisted, or deferred from early to regular.
- A waitlist is worked with a letter of continued interest, which is a real
  and learnable skill.
- **For an international student the process is not over at acceptance.** The
  college issues an **I-20**, the family pays the **SEVIS fee**, and the student
  books and sits a **visa interview at the US Embassy**. Refusals happen, and a
  refused visa in August undoes the entire year. The interview needs
  preparation, and proof of funds needs assembling months earlier.

## What this means for Yakal

Yakal is an Ethiopian company and its students are international applicants.
That changes which parts of this process are hard:

- FAFSA is irrelevant to them and currently appears on every college.
- English proficiency testing is compulsory for them and is barely modelled.
- Financial aid strategy is harder and higher stakes for them than for an
  American student.
- The visa step is theirs alone, is genuinely frightening, and is absent from
  the product entirely.

Naviance, Scoir and the Common App itself are built for American high schools.
None of them handle the Addis to Boston pathway well, because it is not their
customer. That gap is the most defensible thing Yakal could own, and it is
discussed in `90-fix-list.md`.
